3. WHERE OUR 10,145 COMES FROM
================================================================================================
  total utility = (trips served) x (mean utility per trip)
  ours :  4,073 trips x  2.491 km/trip =     10,145
  paper(repro):  4,195 trips x  2.437 km/trip =     10,221

  Both numbers are small because BOTH the request stream is thinned AND
  a driver can only serve one trip at a time. The per-trip utility (~2.5 km
  net of deadhead) is realistic for Manhattan.

================================================================================================
4. WHY THE PAPER'S 95,824 IS NOT PHYSICALLY ACHIEVABLE
================================================================================================
  Paper: mean utility per driver per week = 4,791.19
  At a realistic net utility of 2.49 km per trip, that implies
  1,924 trips per driver per week.

  But the paper's protocol (Sec. 5.2) uses a peak 2-hour window per
  day, so a driver is available at most 14 h in the week.
  Mean Manhattan trip in the test week = 11.3 min, plus
  ~4.5 min deadhead -> at most 53 trips per driver.

  Required 1,924 trips vs physically possible 53
  = 36x over capacity.

  This is explained by the paper's own modelling choice (Sec. 4.3):
  "The proposed method allows an agent (driver) to accept multiple
  requests concurrently" -- and its utility has no time dimension, so
  there is no bound on how much work one driver can absorb in a timestep.
  Their utility therefore aggregates trips a real driver could not perform.

  Our environment enforces occupancy: an assigned driver is unavailable
  for tau(g,s) + tau(s,d) minutes. That single constraint is what makes
  Gap 2 measurable at all, and it is the main reason our totals are
  an order of magnitude smaller. The comparison that matters is therefore
  WITHIN our harness (all six methods on identical settings), which is
  exactly how Table 1 and Table 1b are constructed.

================================================================================================
5. OUR SETUP IS DEMAND-LIMITED BY DESIGN, NOT DATA-LIMITED
================================================================================================
  online driver-slots (5 min each)          63,120
  mean slots consumed per trip                5.74   (measured)
  => fleet capacity this week               11,006 trips
  requests offered                           6,604
  offered load = demand / capacity           0.600   (target 0.6)

  Available (un-thinned) test-week trips: 2,231,191. We deliberately
  do NOT use all of them: 2,231,191 requests against a capacity of
  11,006 would be 203x oversubscribed, every driver would be
  busy every minute, utilisation would pin at 1.0, and Var(utilisation)
  -- the entire Gap 2 metric -- would collapse to zero for every method.
  Confirmed empirically: under the paper's 0.05 rate our run gives 2.6%
  service rate, 0.99 utilisation and 99 of 200 drivers idle.

  The sample rate is solved so offered load = 0.60, i.e. the regime where
  fairness is contested and both gaps are measurable.

================================================================================================
SUMMARY
================================================================================================
  * 100% of the cleaned dataset (10,300,738 trips) builds every model
    component: graph, distances, 48-profile travel times, demand, forecaster.
  * The allocation experiment runs on a calibrated stratified sample, which
    is the same practice as the paper (it uses 0.05).
  * The paper's much larger totals come from two unreported/unphysical
    settings, not from more data: n = 20 drivers (recovered above) and
    unlimited concurrent trips per driver, implying ~40x more trips per
    driver than the hours in its own window allow.
  * Absolute utilities are therefore not comparable across the two papers.
    All conclusions here come from comparisons INSIDE one harness, where
    every method sees identical drivers, requests, distances and times.
